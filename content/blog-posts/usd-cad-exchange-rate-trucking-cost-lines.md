The Bank of Canada put USD/CAD at 1.3989 on November 5. The observation before it, November 4, was 1.3961. As USD CAD exchange rate news goes, trucking costs barely move on a 0.2 per cent shift. What it does is mark the level, and the level sits inside a stack of cost lines a carrier has to live with whether the daily print went up or down.

One US dollar buys 1.3989 Canadian dollars at that reading. Against 1.4085, the 30-day average, the rate is 0.0096 lower, which puts the loonie stronger than its own month. Against the previous day it is weaker. Both of those were true on the same afternoon, and that is the whole difficulty with reading a currency for a living.

## USD CAD exchange rate trucking costs, line by line

Pull a cost sheet apart and the two currencies separate cleanly. Some lines are invoiced in American dollars, some in Canadian, and the exchange rate only reaches the first group.

| Line | Priced in | What sets the number |
|---|---|---|
| Fuel bought south of the line | US dollars | EIA weekly retail survey, $6.292 a gallon, week of 2 November 2026 |
| Fuel bought in Canada | Canadian dollars | NRCan weekly survey, 258.8 cents a litre, print date 3 November 2026 |
| Fuel tax inside that Canadian price | statute, not currency | IFTA matrix, Quebec 0.202 a litre, Ontario 0.09 a litre |
| Tractors, trailers, parts and tires from US suppliers | US dollars | supplier invoice |
| Wages, Canadian insurance, Canadian shop labour | Canadian dollars | payroll and policy |
| Revenue on a US lane | US dollars | the customer |
| Revenue on a Canadian lane | Canadian dollars | the customer |

The rows on the American side are the ones a rate move reprices. Everything else sits still while the loonie moves, which is why a carrier can feel a currency swing on one lane and not on the next one out of the same yard.

Fuel is the biggest line that behaves that way. The dashboard prices fuel at 25 to 35 per cent of operating cost, and it is the one input that prints a new number every week. A parts invoice shows up once a quarter. A fuel receipt shows up every day.

## Where a 0.2 per cent move actually lands

Two carriers, same rate, opposite exposures.

A Canadian carrier running US lanes earns in American dollars and spends a large share of it in Canadian. When the rate rises, US revenue converts into more loonies. The same move makes the American fuel stop, the American tire and the American tractor dearer inside the account that pays for them. Which side wins depends on how much of that truck's cost base sits south of the line, and only the carrier holds that number.

A US carrier running Ontario lanes has the mirror problem. Revenue arrives in American dollars, and the fuel, the driver, the tolls and the shop are Canadian. A stronger loonie makes every one of those Canadian costs heavier in the currency the customer paid in. The rate on the invoice did not change. The margin on it did.

Neither carrier is wrong to watch the rate. Both are wrong to watch it daily. A 0.2 per cent print is inside the noise of what a Monday load pays versus a Thursday one.

## What to do with a 0.2 per cent move

Quote in the currency your cost base sits in, or write the currency into the rate. A lane priced in Canadian dollars while its fuel is bought in American dollars is a rate with a hole in it, and the hole opens wider the longer the contract runs.

Pick a rate for the quarter and book against it. Re-marking a lane to the daily print produces invoices nobody can audit, and small moves tend to cancel across a month more often than they stack.

Fuel where you get paid. On a US lane the customer pays in American dollars, so a fuel stop south of the line keeps the cost in the same currency as the revenue. On a Canadian lane the reverse holds.

Watch the level against the 30-day average, not the overnight tick. At 1.3989 against 1.4085 the loonie is stronger than its own month. That sentence is worth more in a rate conversation than any single day's print.

## The lever that does not care what the loonie does

The gap between provinces is set by tax and by the freight it takes to get the product there. The currency moves the level underneath all of them at once, and it moves the gap between them hardly at all. The national average is 258.8 cents a litre this week. Quebec is dearest at 289.8. The Northwest Territories is cheapest at 226.3. The spread between them is 63.5 cents, on the same survey, printed the same day.

That lever works whichever way the rate goes. A carrier filling at the cheap end of the range is deciding something the exchange rate cannot undo. The [Quebec price tables](https://dashboard.northernmilemedia.com/diesel-prices/quebec/montreal/) and the [Alberta ones](https://dashboard.northernmilemedia.com/diesel-prices/alberta/calgary/) carry the two ends of it.

Tax is the part of that spread nobody routes around. The quarterly IFTA matrix lists Quebec at 0.202 a litre and Ontario at 0.09. Same product in the tank, a different statutory charge on top of it. That line does not move when the loonie moves. It moves when a jurisdiction changes its rate, which is a slower and more permanent kind of change.

## What this week's calendar holds

No forecast here. What is knowable is what printed and when it prints again. The Natural Resources Canada diesel survey is dated 3 November 2026 and refreshes weekly. The exchange rate observation is dated 5 November 2026 and prints daily on business days. The US Energy Information Administration figure is for the week of 2 November 2026 and prints weekly, at $6.292 a gallon nationally.

Those three are the pump side, the conversion side and the American comparison, and each one carries its own print date for a reason. A carrier who quotes off a stale conversion is quoting a number that was true on a different day than the fuel price sitting beside it.

The rate at 1.3989 is a level, not an event. Put it next to your own cost base and the lines that matter separate on their own.
