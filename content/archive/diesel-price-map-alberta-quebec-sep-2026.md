## The Number That Matters More Than The Average

The national average for Canadian diesel sits at 267.1¢/L this week, per Natural Resources Canada's latest print (Tue, Sep 15). But averages lie if you're actually filling up.

Quebec truckers are paying 296.3¢/L. Alberta drivers at 243.1¢/L. That's a **53.2¢/L gap** between two provinces sitting on opposite sides of the country. On a 10,000-litre tank run, that's $5,320 difference. For a fleet doing 50 tanks a month, that's $266,000 — not chump change.

This isn't about supply shortages. All ten index provinces have adequate station counts and refinery access. The gap comes from provincial excise taxes, distribution logistics, and local market structure.

## Why This Spread Exists

Quebec's excise tax on diesel sits above the national average. It's been higher for years, but when combined with freight patterns — fewer cross-provincial hauls terminating in QC compared to ON or AB — the local pump pressure compounds. The province relies more heavily on rail-fed supply chains through Montreal terminals, adding handling steps before the product reaches retail outlets.

Alberta benefits from proximity to domestic production. The province produces crude locally and has refining capacity that feeds domestic demand before any surplus exports south. That structural advantage translates directly to lower retail diesel, even when global oil prices spike.

Ontario falls roughly in the middle. It doesn't produce fuel domestically, and its huge consumption base means it imports from multiple points — refineries in Sarnia, Edmonton shipments, sometimes US border-crossing product. Multiple sources keep competitive pressure on price, but the sheer volume also means any supply disruption gets noticed quickly.
