The Canadian dollar is trading at 1.4162 against the US dollar. That is 4.9 percent weaker than the 1.35 baseline. Directionally this favours Canadian carriers running US freight.

A $3,000 US load converts to $4,249 CAD at today's rate. At 1.35 it converts to $4,050. That is an extra $199 on the same load. The difference is not marginal. A carrier doing 10 US loads a week is looking at roughly $2,000 more per week in Canadian dollar terms compared to the baseline. That is $100,000 a year from exchange rate movement alone.

The loonie has been sliding since late June. It touched 1.4234 on June 24 and has stayed between 1.40 and 1.42 since. The July low was 1.3924. Today's close is the highest in two weeks. The trend line is still pointing toward a weaker dollar.

What is driving it. Canadian rates held steady in July. US rate cuts are not expected until September at the earliest. The rate differential pushes capital toward the higher yielding currency. In this case that is the US dollar. Until that gap narrows, the loonie stays under pressure.

GDP grew 0.3 percent month over month. Year over year the economy is up 1.7 percent. Both numbers are positive. Both are modest. The economy is expanding but it is not accelerating. Freight demand follows GDP with a lag of roughly one quarter. Current growth supports steady volume. It does not support a capacity crunch. If you are pricing lanes, assume demand holds. Do not assume it spikes.

The offset is diesel. The national average sits at 224.8 cents a litre. That is up 36 percent from the 165 cent baseline. Fuel typically eats 25 to 35 percent of operating costs. At 224.8 cents it is at the high end of that range.

British Columbia leads at 233.2. Alberta is the only province under 205 cents at 200.3. The spread from Calgary to Vancouver is 32.9 cents a litre. On that run the fuel cost spread alone is $112 per fill. The weak dollar helps cross border revenue. It does nothing for domestic fuel bills.

Equipment costs are a quiet factor. Most heavy truck parts and tires are priced in US dollars. A weaker loonie makes every replacement part more expensive in CAD terms. Fleets running older equipment with higher maintenance spend feel this first. New truck orders priced in USD are roughly 5 percent more expensive than they were in the spring.

For carriers the net math depends on lane mix. A fleet with 70 percent US freight gets a clear tailwind. A fleet running domestic lanes absorbs the full fuel increase and the higher parts costs. A mixed fleet breaks even or better depending on how many US miles they log.
